- **Score:** 72.7  
- **Topics:** astrochemistry, molecular_clouds, massive_star_formation  
- **Authors:** Tung Wei Goh, Satoya Nakano, Kengo Tachihara, Tohru Ohba  
- **Subjects:** astro-ph.GA  
- **arXiv:** [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41921](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41921)  
- **PDF:** [https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.41921.pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.41921.pdf)  
- **Abstract:** We investigate past star formation in NGC 6357 using Gaia DR3 astrometry and Three-mm Ultimate Mopra Milky Way Survey (ThrUMMS) $^{12}$CO($J=1$--0) data. A Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (HDBSCAN) search independently recovers the previously cataloged cluster G352.8+0.6 in addition to Pismis 24, G353.1+0.6, and G353.2+0.6. Isochrone fitting yields an age of $7.9^{+1.5}_{-1.4}$ Myr for G352.8+0.6, compared with approximately 1--3 Myr for the three young clusters. Assuming constant relative plane-of-sky motion, a linear traceback places G352.8+0.6 closest to their reference center approximately 1.8 Myr ago, comparable to their inferred ages. The $+2$ km s$^{-1}$ CO component overlaps G352.8+0.6 in projection and shows localized depressions in integrated intensity around three spectroscopically identified massive stars belonging to G352.8+0.6. The $-4$ and $+2$ km s$^{-1}$ components show complementary projected distributions and intermediate-velocity CO emission in position--velocity space. Assuming that the $+2$ km s$^{-1}$ component was physically associated with G352.8+0.6 and shared its transverse motion, our principal interpretation is that the two molecular structures interacted around the epoch indicated by the stellar traceback, approximately 1.8 Myr ago. The resulting gas compression may have contributed to the recent episode of clustered and high-mass star formation in NGC 6357 and to the formation of Shell A, which subsequent stellar feedback may have reshaped.

#### [B] How to feed your dragon: the role of turbulence in AGN activity
- **Score:** 70.7  
- **Topics:** turbulence, halo_cgm, molecular_clouds  
- **Authors:** Olga Borodina, Jake S. Bennett, Yueying Ni, Rainer Weinberger, Michaela Hirschmann, Boon Kiat Oh, Lars Hernquist  
- **Subjects:** astro-ph.GA  
- **arXiv:** [https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40788](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40788)  
- **PDF:** [https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.40788.pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.40788.pdf)  
- **Abstract:** Active galactic nuclei (AGN) are thought to be important drivers of galaxy evolution. However, the way AGN jets couple their energy to the interstellar medium (ISM) remains poorly understood. Recent observations have revealed $\sim$kpc-scale jets in galaxies of different morphological types. Here, we use idealized turbulent box simulations with accreting black holes to investigate how jet-ISM interactions and feedback shape the evolution of jets. We show that a turbulent ISM is a key ingredient in both jet launching and jet propagation. Without turbulence, jet feedback initially shuts off the cold gas supply to the black hole and the system enters a steady state with jet power $\leq 10^{39}\,\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}$ for a $10^{6}\,\mathrm{M}_\odot$ black hole. In a turbulent medium, jets fill voids with hot diffuse material which the turbulence then carries away, replenishing the black hole's fuel supply. However, above a critical jet efficiency factor, the jet begins to significantly deplete its fuel supply and enters a self-limiting accretion regime. This critical factor depends on the ISM Mach number, with lower turbulence yielding a lower threshold. Moreover, while the jet power depends on the ambient gas density in the vicinity of the black hole, we find that, at fixed turbulent structure, the morphology and extent of jet propagation are largely insensitive to the average background density; turbulence in the ISM dominates over mean density in setting how far jets propagate, although denser media experience higher total energy injection and stronger heating.

- **Abstract:** We present a statistical study of the cool circumgalactic medium (CGM) around massive post-starburst galaxies, traced by Mg II $\lambda\lambda2796,2803$ absorption in background quasar spectra from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) Data Release 2. Cross-matching ~1.98 million quasar sightlines with luminous red galaxies at $0.4 < z < 0.76$ with stellar masses between $10.6 < \log_{10}(M_*/\mathrm{M}_\odot) < 12$ yields ~143 million galaxy-quasar pairs, 50-60 times more per impact-parameter bin than our previous SDSS-based analysis. We stack the Mg II absorption as a function of projected separation and classify the foreground galaxies using mean-field independent component analysis (MFICA), further subdividing the post-starburst population into sub-samples ordered by time since the starburst. The mean Mg II equivalent width declines with impact parameter for every class. Post-starburst galaxies show the strongest absorption of any spectral class, reaching $W_{\rm MgII} = 1.58 \pm 0.32$ Å within 71 kpc, well above star-forming ($0.69 \pm 0.15$ Å) and quiescent ($0.22 \pm 0.05$ Å) galaxies of comparable stellar mass at the same projected separation. Within the post-starburst phase the absorption profile evolves systematically: the youngest systems show the highest, most centrally concentrated absorption and the steepest radial decline, whereas progressively older systems show weaker inner absorption and enhanced absorption at intermediate radii (~300-1000 kpc). Independent Bagpipes star-formation-history fitting confirms that this ordering corresponds to an ageing sequence. We interpret this evolution as the outward redistribution of cool gas expelled by feedback-driven winds, consistent with characteristic outflow velocities of a few hundred km s$^{-1}$, with a residual inner cool-gas reservoir persisting throughout the post-starburst phase.

- **Abstract:** Blue horizontal branch (BHB) stars serve as good standard candles and kinematic tracers of the Milky Way halo. - **PDF:** [https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.41490.pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.41490.pdf)  
- **Abstract:** Models of bursty star formation in low-mass (10^8-10^9.5 Msun) galaxies at z>4 differ most in the suppressed phases: what drives the decline, how fast star formation falls, and how long it stays low. OASIS Paper I showed that such quiet galaxies are common and old; here we measure how they got there and how their ionized gas responds. We compare Balmer-line star-formation rates (SFRs) with 100-Myr averages from the stellar continuum, in NIRSpec/PRISM spectra of 54 OASIS galaxies at z>4, including three mini-quenched galaxies below the quenching threshold and five lulling galaxies at least 0.6 dex below the star-forming main sequence (SFMS) but still star-forming. Galaxies above the SFMS rise and fade equally often, whereas those below it are mostly fading: the lulling galaxies formed little more than a tenth of their mass in the last 100 Myr, and their decline began tens to ~100 Myr before observation. Suppressed phases thus last of order 100 Myr, as long as or longer than in most simulations, whereas recovery is much faster: few galaxies below the SFMS are rising. The ionized gas tracks these changes in star formation. [OIII]/[OII] falls from galaxies above the SFMS to the lulling ones, even at fixed mass and after dust correction, and follows the recent change in SFR (SFR_10/SFR_100) more closely than the position relative to the SFMS: two rising galaxies below the SFMS are highly ionized. This is expected if fewer and softer ionizing photons than in strongly star-forming galaxies meet gas depleted by earlier star formation. Lulling galaxies show ISM conditions distinct from those of strongly star-forming galaxies. The declines are faster than passive gas exhaustion predicts, favoring feedback that expels the gas or keeps it from forming stars. Quiet phases are thus long, feedback-driven episodes, and the ionized gas traces where a galaxy is in its burst cycle.

Here we report the detection of a star-forming region located in the southern part of Cygnus-X, Sharpless 106 (S106), using the HAWC Observatory.
